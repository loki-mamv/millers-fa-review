Biomechanics & Anatomy

Key Ankle Anatomy

  • Hindfoot: talus, calcaneus, and cuboid
  • Joints: subtalar, calcaneocuboid (CC), talonavicular (TN)
  • Function: primarily inversion and eversion

Ligament Hierarchy (Strength)

ATFL weakest → PTFL strongest
Can lead to avulsion injuries of distal tip of fibula

Gait Cycle Highlights

Gait Mechanics

  • One full stride: heel strike to heel strike
  • Stance phase: heel strike to toe-off (62% of cycle)
  • Swing phase: toe-off to heel strike (38% of cycle)

Key Muscle Actions

  • Swing phase: Anterior tibialis contracts concentrically
    • Loss → footdrop and steppage gait
  • Heel strike: Anterior tibialis contracts eccentrically
    • Controls foot strike rate; footdrop causes loud "slap"
  • Foot flat: Gastrocnemius-soleus contracts eccentrically
    • Controls forward body progression
  • Toe-off: Gastrocnemius-soleus contracts concentrically
    • PTT fires to create rigid lever arm
Windlass Mechanism: Plantar fascia tightens as MTP joints extend → longitudinal arch accentuates → rigid propellant

Foot Positions vs Motions

Hindfoot Mechanics

  • Locked/Inverted: Initial heel strike
  • Unlocked/Everted: Foot flat (ground accommodation)
  • Supinated: Toe-off (rigid lever creation)

Cavovarus Pathology

Failure of hindfoot eversion: increased lateral foot forces
  • Stress fractures (5th metatarsal)
  • Callus formation
  • Ankle instability

Transverse Tarsal Joint

  • Foot flat: Unlocked for shock absorption
  • Toe-off: Locked by PTT firing
  • PTT dysfunction: Limits rigid lever creation
Multiple etiologies of flatfoot: apex usually midfoot- or hindfoot-driven

Hallux Valgus — Pathoanatomy

Radiographic Measurements

Normal Values

Measurement Normal Value Clinical Significance
HVA (Hallux Valgus Angle) <15° Primary deformity assessment
IMA (Intermetatarsal Angle) <9° Determines surgical approach
DMAA (Distal Metatarsal Articular Angle) <10° Joint congruency assessment
Hallux Valgus Interphalangeus <10° Associated congruent deformity
Pronation: leads to rounding of lateral head (should have flat contour)

Hallux Valgus — Surgical Algorithm

Decision Algorithm

IMA ≤13° AND/OR HVA ≤40°

→ Distal osteotomy (e.g., chevron)

IMA ≥13° AND/OR HVA >40°

→ Proximal metatarsal osteotomy

Hyperlaxity or 1st TMT instability

→ Arthrodesis of 1st TMT

Mandatory in All Cases

Soft tissue release should be done in ALL scenarios

High Recurrence Risk

  • Undercorrection of IMA
  • Isolated soft tissue correction
  • Isolated medial eminence resection
  • Persistent lateral sesamoid subluxation
  • No soft tissue correction with osteotomy
Juvenile/Adolescent: Recurrence is most common complication

Hallux Valgus — Special Considerations

Juvenile Hallux Valgus

IMA >13° with elevated DMAA

Proximal osteotomy OR open-wedge medial cuneiform osteotomy

PLUS medial closed-wedge distal osteotomy (biplanar chevron)

→ Tilts joint back into correct position

Nerve Injury Risk

Medial approaches: Risk injury to mediodorsal cutaneous nerve branch of superficial peroneal nerve

Hallux Varus (Iatrogenic)

Causes:

  • Fibular sesamoidectomy
  • Overcorrection of IMA
  • Over-resection of medial eminence
  • Excessive lateral soft tissue release

Treatment (flexible deformity):

  • Medial capsulotomy
  • Abductor hallucis tenotomy
  • Extensor tendon transfer (EHL or EHB)
  • May augment with suture button techniques

Lesser Toe Deformities

Pathophysiology

  • Extrinsic muscles overpower intrinsics:
    • EDL drives MTP extension
    • FDL drives PIP/DIP flexion
  • FDL contracture: dynamic component of hammer toe

Crossover Toe (2nd Toe)

Key component: Plantar plate disruption

Plus: Lateral collateral ligament attenuation

Most sensitive test: Anterior drawer test of lesser MTP joint

Treatment Algorithm

Deformity Type Flexible Fixed
Hammer/Claw Toes Flexor-to-extensor transfer OR FDL tenotomy ± PIP arthroplasty PIP arthrodesis
Plantar Plate Injury Toe taping + metatarsal pads Surgical repair (high-level athletes)
Claw toes: Often neurogenic (CMT) or compartment syndrome complication

Ankle Sprains

Lateral Ligament Anatomy

Physical Examination

  • Anterior drawer test: Checks ATFL competence
  • Inversion in plantarflexion: Also evaluates ATFL
  • Inversion in dorsiflexion: Evaluates CFL

Grading & Treatment

  • RICE protocol: Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation
  • Physical therapy: Balance, proprioception, peroneal strengthening
  • Functional bracing + neuromuscular training: Decreases reinjury more than training alone

Chronic Instability

Brostrom procedure: Primary lateral ligament reconstruction

Consider peroneal tendon pathology in chronic lateral ankle pain

PTT Dysfunction / Pes Planus

Clinical Presentation

  • Symptoms: Medial ankle pain, progressive arch collapse, lateral hindfoot pain
  • Physical exam: Asymmetric hindfoot valgus, depressed arch, abducted forefoot
  • Key test: Inability to perform single-limb heel rise
  • Too-many-toes sign: Visible lateral toes when viewing from behind

Johnson & Strom/Myerson Classification

Stage Description Treatment
Stage 1 Tendinosis, no deformity Conservative
Stage 2 Flexible flatfoot, hindfoot valgus PTT repair/reconstruction + lateral column lengthening
Stage 3 Fixed/rigid deformity Double or triple arthrodesis
Stage 4 Ankle valgus (deltoid insufficiency) Consider deltoid reconstruction or ankle replacement
Spring ligament: Primary static stabilizer of TN joint (superomedial band most important - 70%)
Most common cause of adult-acquired flatfoot is PTT dysfunction

PTT Dysfunction — Surgical Management

Operative Indications

6+ months of failed conservative measures

Surgical Procedures

  • Lateral column lengthening: Addresses hindfoot valgus, improves longitudinal arch
  • Cotton osteotomy: Dorsal open-wedge of cuneiform to plantarflex 1st ray (corrects forefoot varus)
  • Gastrocnemius recession: Often contracted (originates from posterior femur)

Surgical Approaches

  • Severely abducted deformities: May need all-medial approach
  • Advantage: Limits wound-healing issues vs sinus tarsi approach
  • Cadaveric studies: >90% joint visualization from medial approach alone

Complications

Subtalar malunion in valgus: May require takedown and revision arthrodesis
Contraindication: Smokers for four-corner fusion

Achilles Disorders

Classification

  • Insertional tendinosis: At bone-tendon junction
  • Noninsertional tendinosis: 2-6 cm proximal to insertion

Physical Examination

  • Silfverskiöld test: Evaluates contracture source
    • Lack of tension on Achilles → posterior capsule likely source
  • Thompson test: Calf squeeze for rupture detection
  • Noninsertional: Fusiform thickening 2-6 cm proximal

Conservative Management

  • Heavy-load eccentric strengthening (highest success rate)
  • Activity modification, heel lifts
  • Silicone heel sleeves/pads
  • Physical therapy

Acute Rupture Management

Initial: Immobilization with foot in plantarflexion

Achilles Rupture — Surgical Management

Chronic Rupture Treatment Algorithm

Gap After Débridement Treatment
<2 cm End-to-end repair
2-5 cm V-Y reconstruction
>5 cm FHL transfer + possible turndown

FHL Transfer Indications

  • Large gaps (>5 cm) in chronic ruptures
  • May need gastrocnemius recession/lengthening
  • Takes tension off anterior tibial tendon reconstruction

Stenosis Location

FHL stenosis: Between posterolateral and posteromedial tubercles of talus

Related Injuries

Plantaris rupture: Similar mechanism to Achilles but managed with tall boot initially

Diabetic Foot

Neuropathy Assessment

Semmes-Weinstein 5.07 monofilament (10 g): Inability to sense = neuropathy
Loss of protective sensation: 30% risk of ulcer development

Glucose Control

  • A1c measurement: Ideal assessment (past 3 months)
  • Elevated A1c: Increases failure risk of transmetatarsal amputation

Vascular Assessment

  • Peripheral vascular disease: Present in 60-70% with DM >10 years
  • Falsely elevated ABI: Due to vascular calcification
  • TcPO2 >40 mmHg: Predictive of healing

Ischemia-Based Classification

Grade Description
Grade A Normal vascularity
Grade B Ischemia without gangrene
Grade C Partial forefoot gangrene
Grade D Complete foot gangrene

Diabetic Foot — Ulcer Management

Wagner Classification & Treatment

Grade Description Treatment
Grade 0 At-risk foot Extra-depth shoe + pressure relief insoles
Grade 1 Superficial, no bone In-office débridement + total contact cast (TCC)
Grade 2 Deep with soft tissue exposure Operative débridement → dressing changes/TCC
Grade 3 Osteomyelitis/abscess + exposed bone Surgical bone/soft tissue débridement → dressing changes/TCC

Special Considerations

  • Dorsal toe ulcers: Best treated with flexor tenotomies
  • Plantar hallux IP ulcers: Failed TCC → Keller arthroplasty
  • Plastazote lining: Closed cell, lightweight, shock-absorbing
Treatment often requires: Achilles lengthening to prevent recurrence + toe deformity correction

Charcot Arthropathy (Neuroarthropathy)

Clinical Presentation

  • Symptoms: Swelling, warmth, redness, deformity
  • Pain: Present in up to 50% of patients
  • Most common site: Midfoot, then ankle, then hindfoot

Eichenholtz Classification

Stage Description Treatment
Development Acute inflammation Non-weight-bearing TCC
Coalescence Decreased inflammation Continue TCC
Consolidation Healing, remodeling Gradual weight-bearing

Pathophysiology

TNF-α and IL-1β → increased NF-κB → increased osteoclast production → bone loss

Timeline

  • Acute phase resolution: TCC for 4-6 weeks
  • Fragmentation to consolidation: 6-18 months
Initial treatment: Non-weight-bearing status with TCC

Diabetic Foot — Surgical Considerations

Osteomyelitis

  • Diagnosis: Labeled WBC scan or dual Tc/In scan (more sensitive than Tc alone)
  • Incidence: 67% of foot ulcerations reaching bone
  • Treatment: Antibiotics + surgical débridement

Charcot Arthrodesis

Unstable/unbraceable deformities

Tibiotalocalcaneal (TTC) arthrodesis

  • Braceable/plantigrade foot
  • Reduces ulceration risk
  • Intramedullary rod preferred (vs external fixator)

Amputation Levels

  • Syme: Requires intact heel pad
  • Chopart: Transfer anterior tibialis to talus (prevent varus) + Achilles lengthening (prevent equinus)

Poor Healing Predictors

  • Total protein <6.0 g/dL
  • WBC <1500 cells/mm³
  • Albumin <2.5 g/dL

Lisfranc Injuries — Anatomy

Anatomic Stability

Lisfranc Ligament Components

Component Relative Strength Clinical Significance
Interosseous Strongest & stiffest Primary stabilizer
Plantar Intermediate Secondary support
Dorsal Weakest Often torn first
Keystone configuration: 2nd metatarsal base fits into mortise formed by recessed middle cuneiform
Roman arch: Coronal plane configuration provides stability

Lisfranc Injuries — Diagnosis

Mechanism & Presentation

  • Indirect: Axial loading of plantarflexed foot (most common)
  • Direct: Motor vehicle accidents, crush injuries
  • Clinical: Severe pain, inability to bear weight, marked swelling

Key Physical Finding

Plantar ecchymosis should raise suspicion for TMT/Lisfranc injury

Radiographic Diagnosis

  • Pathognomonic: Lateral translation of 2nd metatarsal relative to middle cuneiform
  • Fleck sign: Small bony avulsion from 2nd metatarsal base in 1st intermetatarsal space
  • Stress views: Weight-bearing, abduction stress, single-limb views
CT and MRI: Valuable for detecting occult fractures and preoperative planning

Lisfranc Injuries — Treatment

Emergency Management

Skin tenting: Urgent reduction needed to reduce compartment syndrome risk

Treatment Algorithm

Nonoperative (rare)

No displacement on weight-bearing/stress radiographs + no bony injury on CT

ORIF (standard)

Any displacement on radiographs OR evidence of bony injury

  • Anatomic reduction mandatory
  • Open reduction often required
  • Fixation: proximal to distal, medial to lateral

Fixation Strategy

Column Fixation Removal
Medial/Middle (1st, 2nd, 3rd TMT) Screw fixation or dorsal plating 4 months
Lateral (4th, 5th TMT) Temporary K-wire fixation 6 weeks
Dorsal plating: Less iatrogenic articular damage, lower malreduction risk

Lisfranc Injuries — Complications & Alternatives

Primary Arthrodesis

Indications:

  • Purely ligamentous high-energy injury
  • Significant intraarticular comminution
  • Chronic injury patterns

Advantages:

  • Reduced hardware removal rates
  • Reduced costs vs ORIF
  • Similar patient-reported outcomes

Complications of Missed/Poor Treatment

Traumatic planovalgus deformity or posttraumatic arthritis
→ Salvage: Open reduction + midfoot arthrodesis

Associated Injuries

  • Cuboid injuries (nutcracker): From abduction force
  • Proximal variant: Forces through intercuneiform joint
  • Compartment syndrome: Risk with severe displacement
Anatomic reduction is most predictive of good clinical results

Ankle Fractures — Classification & Evaluation

Lauge-Hansen Classification

Position of foot (1st word) + Direction of force (2nd word)

Pattern Characteristics Special Notes
Supination-Adduction Vertical medial malleolus + low transverse fibular Associated with 2nd orthopaedic injuries
SER (most common) Oblique fibula at syndesmosis level Stress test determines stability
Pronation-Abduction Comminuted fibula above syndesmosis Obvious syndesmotic disruption
Pronation-ER Oblique fibula above syndesmosis Slight syndesmotic widening

Danis-Weber Classification

  • A (infrasyndesmotic): Stable, rarely operative
  • B (transsyndesmotic): Variably unstable
  • C (suprasyndesmotic): Inherently unstable
Log splitter injury: Talus displaced into syndesmotic space → 54-85% posttraumatic arthritis risk

Ankle Fractures — Management Principles

Key Radiographic Measurements

At 10 mm above plafond:

  • Tibiofibular clear space: <6 mm (most predictive of syndesmotic disruption)
  • Tibiofibular overlap: >6 mm (AP), >1 mm (mortise)

Critical Concepts

1 mm lateral talar shift = 42% decrease in tibiotalar contact area

Deltoid Ligament Assessment

  • External rotation/gravity stress radiographs
  • Medial clear space widening indicates deep deltoid disruption
  • Implies unstable fracture pattern

Posterior Malleolus

  • Contributes 70% syndesmotic stability
  • ORIF indications: >25% articular surface OR >2 mm displacement
  • Recent emphasis: Displacement more important than size
Braking recovery: Average 9 weeks after operative ankle fracture fixation

Ankle Fractures — Syndesmosis & Complications

Syndesmotic Fixation

Flexible fixation preferred:

Suture button devices show superior outcomes vs screw fixation

Key Technical Points

  • Most common complication: Malreduction (up to 52%)
  • Clamp positioning: 15° external rotation relative to foot
  • No benefit to screw removal (retained/broken screws not detrimental)
  • Dorsiflexion not critical during reduction

Special Populations

Diabetic Patients:

  • Non-weight-bearing period: Double that of non-diabetics (3 months)
  • Additional fixation: Consider syndesmotic ladder or external fixation
  • Biggest risk factor: Presence of neuropathy
  • Amputation rate: Up to 30%

Malunion Treatment

  • Fibular osteotomy to restore length
  • Medial gutter débridement
  • Syndesmosis reconstruction

Talus Fractures

Anatomical Considerations

  • Blood supply: Mainly from posterior tibial artery
  • No muscular attachments
  • >50% articular cartilage coverage
  • Retrograde flow to talar body

Hawkins Classification (Talar Neck)

Type Description AVN Rate Blood Supply
Type I Nondisplaced Low 2/3 vessels intact
Type II ST joint disruption Moderate 1/3 vessels intact (deltoid)
Type III ST + ankle disruption High (~50%) Complete compromise
Type IV + TN joint disruption Very High Head and body at risk

Key Management Points

  • Immediate reduction for dislocations
  • Preserve deltoid artery (only remaining supply in Type II)
  • Most common complication: Subtalar arthritis
Hawkins sign: Subchondral lucency at 6-8 weeks = revascularization (good prognostic sign)

Calcaneus Fractures

Mechanism & Pathology

  • High-energy trauma: Fall from height, MVA
  • "Blow-out" of lateral wall: Subfibular impingement
  • 20% peroneal tendon dislocation
  • Evaluate for: Vertebral fractures (concomitant injury)

Sanders CT Classification

Treatment Guidelines

Sanders Type Treatment Approach
Type I Conservative Cast immobilization
Types II-III ORIF vs Conservative Extensile lateral or sinus tarsi
Type IV ORIF + primary ST fusion Extensile lateral

Calcaneus Fractures — Outcomes & Complications

Radiographic Measurements

  • Böhler angle: Normal 25-40°
  • Critical angle of Gissane: Normal 120-145°
  • Broden views: Assess posterior facet displacement

Operative vs Conservative Outcomes

Better outcomes with surgery in:

  • Significant intraarticular displacement
  • Flattened Böhler angle
  • Female sex
  • Age <29 years
  • Non-workers' compensation

Complications

  • Delayed wound healing: 25-30% with extensile approach
  • Deep infection: 1-4%
  • 50% decreased ST ROM regardless of treatment
  • Posttraumatic arthritis: Common

Technical Considerations

Wrinkle sign: Wait for skin wrinkles over lateral hindfoot before surgery (swelling resolved)
FHL at risk: During lateral-to-medial screw placement at sustentaculum level

Pilon Fractures

Demographics & Mechanism

  • 4th decade of life, male predominance
  • High-energy mechanism: Fall from height, MVA
  • Axial load to tibial plafond

Common Fragment Patterns

  • Medial fragment: Deltoid ligament attachment
  • Volkmann fragment: Posterior tibiofibular ligament
  • Chaput fragment: Anterior tibiofibular ligament

Treatment Principles

Staged Protocol (Standard):

  1. Primary external fixation (fibular fixation not necessary)
  2. Wait for swelling resolution
  3. Delayed ORIF when soft tissues permit

ORIF Principles

  • Anatomic reduction and rigid fixation of articular surface
  • Preserve tibial length
  • Reconstruct metaphyseal shell
  • Bone graft metaphyseal defects
  • Bridge plating technique acceptable
Immediate ORIF: Unacceptable wound complication rates

⚡ Quick-Fire Facts

  • ATFL weakest → PTFL strongest ankle ligament
  • 62% stance, 38% swing phase in gait
  • Most common cause adult flatfoot: PTT dysfunction
  • Lisfranc interosseous component strongest
  • 1mm lateral talar shift = 42% contact loss
  • Braking recovery: 9 weeks post ankle fracture ORIF
  • Sanders Type IV = primary ST fusion

🎯 Operative Indications (Know These!)

  • Hallux valgus: IMA >13° = proximal osteotomy
  • PTT dysfunction: 6+ months failed conservative
  • Lisfranc: ANY displacement = ORIF
  • Ankle fractures: Unstable patterns, syndesmotic disruption
  • Calcaneus: Sanders II-III controversial, IV needs fusion
